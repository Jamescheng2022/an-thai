When a Pile Integrity Report Says “Pass” but the Waveform Looks Odd: Seven Questions

By AN-THAI Engineering · 2026-10-28

Why a “Pass” label is not the whole answer

A low-strain impact test can help screen a pile for possible integrity-related changes, but interpretation depends on pile material, geometry, accessibility, site conditions and signal quality. A report that simply says “Pass” or “No significant anomaly detected” may leave an owner’s engineer unsure what evidence supports the judgment. Equally, an unfamiliar waveform is not proof of a defect.

The useful question is whether the data, site context and interpretation can be traced to the particular pile tested. The steps below help organize a technical review; they are not a universal acceptance checklist or a substitute for the project engineer’s assessment.

1. Is this the right engineering question?

Low-strain impact methods examine wave response for possible integrity-related anomalies. They do not directly determine geotechnical bearing capacity. If the decision concerns allowable load or load–settlement behaviour, the project engineer must identify an appropriate different test or analysis. A positive integrity-screening conclusion is not a load-test certificate.

2. Can the signal be matched to the correct pile?

Check the pile ID, location, type, geometry, construction record and test date. Match the schedule, as-built drawings, field log, image or file name and summary table. When identifiers disagree, reconcile the records before drawing conclusions. This is a data-control issue even if the visible waveform looks familiar.

3. Does the report describe how data were acquired?

Look for the pile-head access and preparation, impact and sensor locations, instrumentation, and any relevant site constraints or instrument checks. Poor coupling or unsuitable impact conditions can affect the record. Meaningful deviations from the agreed test procedure belong in the technical record, not behind a one-word result.

4. Are the original signal records reviewable?

Ask for the evidence and explanation required by the agreed scope. Where access rights allow, obtain representative raw traces with readable axes, clear pile references and an account of repeatability or rejected measurements where relevant. A single clean screenshot may conceal acquisition issues; a single awkward trace may justify another look rather than an immediate failure verdict.

Ask which assumptions were used when interpreting reflection features, including wave-speed estimates and whether a pile-toe reflection could reasonably be identified. Some pile and soil combinations do not produce an unambiguous end reflection.

5. Were geometry, installation history and ground conditions considered?

Changes in pile cross-section, material, surrounding ground or installation method can complicate a response. Review the relevant drawings, concreting or driving records, ground profile and comparable nearby pile evidence, if available. The interpretation should connect the signal to these conditions, not treat a waveform as a stand-alone photograph of a pile.

6. Does the conclusion separate evidence from uncertainty?

Look for a clear distinction between signal quality, observed features, possible explanations and the responsible engineer’s judgment. “Inconclusive,” “possible anomaly” and “confirmed defect” are not interchangeable. An ambiguous result can call for better acquisition, document reconciliation or a suitable complementary investigation; there is no one follow-up method for every foundation.

7. What method and acceptance basis govern the decision?

The report should identify the responsible testing organization and reviewer, the contractually adopted method and edition where specified, and project-specific decision or escalation criteria. ASTM D5882-16 was withdrawn in 2025 with no replacement listed on its official product page; it must not be presented as a currently active standard. Do not substitute a generic web checklist for the project specification.

If the report includes a statement of conformity, ask which rule was used to reach it and which relevant uncertainty or limitation was considered. ILAC G8:09/2019 provides general guidance on decision rules in laboratory reporting; it is not a universal pile acceptance criterion and does not establish anyone’s accreditation.

A practical handover request

Send the pile schedule and drawing references, accurate pile ID, relevant construction and ground records, the original test report, and the trace or statement in question. Access to raw instrument exports may require agreement with the testing team. Request written reconciliation of the evidence, assumptions and proposed next step rather than asking for a label change without explanation.

Frequently asked questions

Does an acceptable low-strain integrity report prove pile bearing capacity?

No. It does not directly determine geotechnical bearing capacity; the project engineer must select an appropriate capacity assessment when that is the actual question.

Should one unusual reflection automatically mean a pile has failed?

No. First check trace quality, repeatability, identification and site context; the responsible engineer determines any follow-up.

Can any reviewer demand raw instrument files?

File disclosure depends on the contract, testing scope and agreed review rights. Request the evidence needed to understand the decision and clarify access with the test provider.
